About the project

Mathematics, applied to a mountain range

Our project explores how mathematics can be used in the real world, with the Andes Mountains as our focus. We look at biodiversity, at the mathematical models that help us understand ecosystems and animal populations, and at the shape of the mountains themselves.

The animals

Three animals, one mountain range

Our documentary follows three animals that live high in the Andes. Each is at risk, and each is linked to the others through what it eats and what eats it.

Endangered

Chinchilla

Chinchilla lanigera and Chinchilla chinchilla

60 hairs can grow from a single follicle

A small rodent of rocky, high-altitude slopes in the Andes, now rare in the wild, with only a few colonies left, mostly in Chile. It has some of the densest fur of any land mammal, which kept it warm in the cold, and nearly wiped it out when it was hunted for the fur trade. It eats grasses, seeds and other plants.

Endangered

Andean mountain cat

Leopardus jacobita

< 2,500 adults are thought to remain

One of the rarest cats in the world. It hunts on rocky, treeless slopes above about 3,000 metres, using its long, ringed tail for balance. Its main prey are mountain viscachas, with chinchillas and other small rodents too, so it depends on healthy rodent populations.

Vulnerable

Andean condor

Vultur gryphus

3.3 m wingspan, at the most

One of the largest flying birds on Earth. It rides mountain air for hours without flapping, and feeds mostly on carrion, which means it recycles what other animals leave behind. It breeds very slowly, raising at most one chick every two years, so losses are hard to make up.

Conservation status follows the IUCN Red List. Check iucnredlist.org for the latest assessment.

Simpson's Diversity Index

Simpson's Diversity Index measures how diverse a place is. It takes into account both the number of species present and how many animals there are of each. As the number of species and their evenness go up, so does diversity.

Simpson's Diversity Index SDI = 1 − ∑ n ( n − 1 ) N ( N − 1 )

n is the number of animals of one species. N is the number of animals of all species together. The index runs from 0 (no diversity) towards 1 (very diverse). It is the chance that two animals picked at random are different species.

      How does the index work?

      Imagine picking two animals at random, without putting the first back. If the area has just one species, they must be the same kind. If it has many species with similar numbers, they are very likely to be different.

      For one species, n(n − 1) counts the ordered pairs of animals that are the same species. Adding that up for every species, and dividing by the N(N − 1) pairs you could pick in total, gives the chance that a random pair matches. Subtracting from 1 gives the chance that they differ, which is the index.

      "Effective species" is 1 divided by the matching chance: the number of equally common species that would give the same diversity. "Evenness" compares that with the number of species actually present, so 1 means every species is equally common.

      Predators and prey

      The Lotka–Volterra model describes two populations that depend on each other: prey, which breed but get eaten, and predators, which eat prey and starve without them. The result is a cycle. When prey are plentiful, predators thrive. Then predators eat too many prey, so the predators starve, and the prey recover.

      dx dt = α x − β x y dy dt = δ x y − γ y

      x is the prey (think chinchillas or viscachas) and y the predators (think mountain cats). A textbook model with made-up numbers, not real data.

      Model

      The Andes as a Gaussian peak

      A mountain looks complicated, but its shape can be described with one equation. A Gaussian peak is the bell curve of statistics, turned into a hill: highest at the summit and sloping away smoothly on every side. Stretch it further in one direction than the other and its contour lines become ellipses, which gives an elliptical peak, a good model of a ridge. Add several peaks together and you have a mountain range.

      One peak h ( x , y ) = H · e − ( u 2 2 σ 1 2 + v 2 2 σ 2 2 )

      H is the height of the summit above the base. σ₁ and σ₂ are how far the mountain spreads along and across its ridge (in km). u and v are the east–west and north–south distances from the summit, turned to line up with the ridge. For a whole range, add the peaks together on top of a base height: h(x, y) = base + h₁ + h₂ + …
